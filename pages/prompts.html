Write to familiesA plain-language message parents can act on

Turn rough notes into a message families can act on.

Prompt 
I'm a college and career advisor at (school name). Turn my notes below into a message for parents and guardians. Many will read it on a phone, and some read English as a second language, so use plain, everyday words and spell out any acronyms (like FAFSA). Start with what families need to do and by when. Keep it under 150 words and end with how to reach me. If anything is missing or unclear in my notes, write [NEED: ___] instead of guessing.

This will go out as: (text / email / flyer / school app post)

My notes: (what's happening, date, time, place, what to bring, deadline)
Why it works
Naming the audience and the format changes the result. A text needs to be much shorter than an email, and "read on a phone" pushes the AI toward short lines.
Try next
  • "Make it shorter for a text message."
  • "Write a Spanish version." Have a fluent speaker check any translation before it goes out.

Reach out to employersAn invite with a clear ask and time commitment

Draft an invite or follow-up for a speaker, site visit, or job shadow.

Prompt 
I'm a college and career advisor at (school name) in (town), working with Heartland Forward's rootEd program. Write a short email inviting (contact name, title, business name) to (speak at career day / host a site visit / offer a job shadow) on (date or date range).

Say why our students would benefit from their field: (one line on why you thought of this business). Say what we're asking, how much of their time it takes, and what we'll handle for them: (for example, 30 minutes; we provide transportation and supervision). End with one clear ask and a reply-by date of (date).

Keep it friendly and under 150 words, with no flattery or buzzwords. Give me two subject line options. If I left out something they'd need to know before saying yes, write [NEED: ___].
Why it works
Busy owners say yes when they can see the time commitment and why you picked them. Generic invites get ignored.
Try next
  • "Write a polite follow-up for if they haven't replied in a week."
  • "Write a thank-you note after the visit that mentions (one thing students got out of it)."

Prep for a student meetingQuestions to ask, and what you might be assuming

Sort your notes into good questions before you sit down.

Remove names and identifying details first. See what not to include.

Prompt 
I'm a college and career advisor preparing to meet with a (grade level) student about (topic, for example, deciding between a 2-year program and a 4-year college). My notes are below, with names and identifying details removed.

Give me:
1. Five open-ended questions that get the student talking, not yes/no questions
2. Any assumptions I seem to be making about this student
3. What I don't know yet that could change my advice
4. One or two realistic next steps we could agree on by the end of the meeting

Be direct. If my notes lean toward a conclusion, tell me what would make that conclusion wrong.

My notes: (paste notes, no names or identifying details)
Why it works
Asking for assumptions and "what would make this wrong" stops the AI from agreeing with your first read on a student. More on the agreement trap.

Before you paste

What to keep out of AI tools.

What not to includeKeep student information out of every prompt

Leave these out of every prompt. It matters most when you prep for a student meeting.

  • Names, initials, student ID numbers, or birthdates.
  • Details that identify a student in a small school even without a name, like "the junior whose dad runs the feed store" or "our only student on the robotics team."
  • Grades, test scores, or attendance tied to one student.
  • IEP, 504, health, or mental health information.
  • Immigration status, family income, financial aid details, custody, or discipline records.

Describe the situation in general terms instead, like "a junior interested in nursing who's worried about cost."

For the other prompts

  • Family messages: don't put one student's information in a message going to many families.
  • Employer emails: a business name and contact are fine. Don't name the students going on a job shadow until you have permission.

If your school or district has an approved AI tool or policy, use that tool and follow that policy.
