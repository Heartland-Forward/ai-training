Understanding AI

What AI is, how chatbots work, and how to get good results from them.

Updated November 2026

The big picture

The basics, for anyone new to AI.

What AI isSoftware that learns from examples

AI is software that learns patterns from lots of examples, then uses them to make a guess about something new.

Most software follows rules a person wrote out step by step. AI works out its own rules from data. Show it thousands of emails marked "spam" and it learns what spam looks like. Show it years of traffic and it learns when the highway backs up.

What it does

  • Recognizes faces, voices, handwriting, and objects in photos.
  • Predicts tomorrow's weather, a late bus, or which students may need a check-in.
  • Recommends the next video, song, or product.
  • Creates text, images, and audio. This is generative AI, covered next.

All four are guesses based on patterns. They're often right and sometimes wrong, so a person still makes the call when it matters.

AI vs. generative AISorting and predicting vs. making something new

Generative AI is the kind of AI that makes new things, like a paragraph, a picture, or a song.

Traditional AI Generative AI
What it does Sorts, predicts, and recommends Writes, draws, and talks
What you get A label, a score, or a pick A first draft
Examples Spam filters, fraud alerts, streaming picks ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, image makers

Chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini run on large language models (LLMs). An LLM is generative AI trained on a huge amount of text. Most of this page is about these chatbots, since they're the tools you'll use at work.

Where you already see AIOn your phone, at school, and in the jobs students want

You use AI every day, even if you don't call it that.

  • On your phone: face unlock, autocorrect, voice assistants, and photo search ("show me pictures of my dog").
  • At home: streaming and shopping picks, spam filters, and map apps that route you around traffic.
  • At school: live captions in video calls, grammar suggestions, and the feeds your students scroll every night.
  • At work in the jobs students want: hospitals use it to read scans, banks to flag fraud, farms to watch crops, and factories to catch defects.

How chatbots work

What happens when you type to ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.

The "zip file" of the internetA compressed snapshot of a lot of text

A chatbot is a compressed snapshot of a huge amount of text, trained to answer like a helpful assistant.

During pre-training, the model reads a vast amount of the internet and squeezes the patterns into its network. During post-training, people rate its answers so it learns to act like an assistant. When you ask it something, it draws on that snapshot.

PromptsWhat you type or say to the AI

A prompt is what you type or say to the AI: a question, a request, or a task.

Vague prompts get vague answers. Say who it's for, what you need, and how long it should be, and the answer gets much better. You can also attach files, photos, or links.

Treat it like a conversation with a new coworker. If the first answer misses, tell it what to change.

Try it: See ready-to-use prompts for advising work.

TokensHow AI reads text

AI reads text in small chunks called tokens: pieces of words, whole words, or punctuation.

A typical sentence is 10 to 20 tokens. Tokens matter because every AI has a limit on how many it can hold at once, which leads to the context window.

Try it: Paste a sentence into Tiktokenizer and watch it split.

The context windowThe AI's memory for one chat

The context window is everything the AI can see in your current chat: your messages, its replies, and any files.

  • The AI remembers what's in the current chat.
  • A new chat starts with a clean slate.
  • Very long chats can push early details out, and answers get worse.

Why AI gets things wrongIt predicts words; it doesn't look facts up

AI predicts the most likely next words. It doesn't look facts up, so it can sound sure and still be wrong.

Your phone's predictive text works the same way, at a much smaller scale. Three things trip chatbots up:

  • Made-up facts. People call these hallucinations: a confident answer with a wrong date, name, or dollar amount.
  • Old information. Each model learned from data up to a cutoff date, so it may not know this year's deadlines or job openings.
  • Local details. It knows common facts and often misses your school's policies or local employers.

Check anything that matters against the original source before you share it. Web search, below, helps with the last two.

What they can do

Beyond typing a question and reading the answer.

Voice, images, and filesMore ways to talk to it than typing

You can talk to a chatbot, show it a photo, or hand it a file. It all becomes tokens in the same chat.

  • Voice: tap the microphone and speak. ChatGPT Voice's "Live" option holds a real back-and-forth conversation, which works well for mock interviews.
  • Images: upload a screenshot, a flyer, or a photo of a handout and ask about it.
  • Files: upload a document or spreadsheet and ask it to summarize, compare, or pull out dates.

Thinking modeSlower answers for harder problems

Thinking mode (some tools call it reasoning) makes the AI work through a problem step by step before it answers.

Turn it on for planning, comparing options, or anything with numbers. Skip it for quick questions and brainstorming, where speed matters more. It takes longer and uses more of your limit.

Web search and toolsCurrent information, and math it can trust

Modern chatbots can search the web and run code, which fixes some of what they get wrong on their own.

  • Web search pulls in current pages, so the AI can answer about this year's deadlines and cite where it found them. Some sites block AI, so it can still miss things.
  • Code lets the AI do real math, analyze a spreadsheet, or make a chart instead of guessing at numbers.

Try it: Ask for sources, then click a couple to check them.

Deep researchA research assistant that works for minutes, not seconds

Deep research runs dozens of web searches, reads the results, and writes you a report with citations.

It takes 5 to 30 minutes and uses more of your limit, so save it for questions like "Which scholarships fit first-generation students going into nursing?"

Where to find it

  • Claude: "Research." Click + below the message box. Paid plans, with web search on.
  • ChatGPT: "Deep research" in the + menu, or type /Deepresearch. Limited on the free plan.
  • Gemini: "Deep Research." Limited for everyone, higher on paid plans.
  • Perplexity: "Research." Limited on the free plan.

Features that save time

Ways to stop starting from scratch every time.

ProjectsStop re-explaining yourself

A Project is a folder of files and standing instructions that every chat inside it already knows.

Load it once with who you are, who you serve, and the files you keep re-pasting. Every new chat in that Project starts with all of it.

  • One per school year: deadlines, checklists, and last year's timeline.
  • One per employer partner: visit notes and what they offered.
  • One per newsletter or update: past issues and a short style note.

Where to find it

  • Claude: Projects, on every plan.
  • ChatGPT: Projects. Project instructions override your general ones.
  • Gemini: Notebooks, in the sidebar. They hold sources, instructions, and chats, and work on school Google accounts unless the school turns them off.

ArtifactsDocuments and tools that open beside the chat

When the AI makes something substantial, like a tracker or a handout, it can open it in a panel beside the chat.

Ask for a change and the document updates in place. A FAFSA deadline tracker or a parent explainer takes a sentence and about a minute.

It's a first draft. The dates and dollar amounts came from the AI, so check them before you use it.

Where to find it

  • Claude: Artifacts. Opens on its own when it fits.
  • ChatGPT: Writing blocks and code blocks, inside the reply. They replaced Canvas.
  • Gemini: Canvas. Click Add files, then Canvas.

SkillsTeach it your process once

A Skill is a saved set of instructions for one kind of task, which the AI uses whenever you ask for that task.

If you've explained the same process twice, make it a Skill: your employer follow-up format, your rules for writing to families, or what to pull before a planning meeting. You can ask the AI to draft the Skill for you.

Where to find it

  • Claude: Skills, on every plan. Used on their own when your request matches.
  • ChatGPT: Skills, under Plugins. Business, Enterprise, Healthcare, and Edu workspaces only.
  • Gemini: Gems for now, which you pick by hand. Google is switching Gems to skills, with school accounts in 2027.

ConnectorsLet the AI read, and sometimes act in, your other apps

A connector links the AI to an app you already use, like your email, calendar, or shared drive.

Instead of pasting in an email thread, you can ask "What did the counselor team decide about FAFSA night?" and the AI looks for itself.

Some connectors can act for you. Claude's Google connectors can send email, change calendar events, and move files, and ask your approval first. ChatGPT's apps can take actions too. Read every approval request before you click it.

Check with your school first. Connectors touch school email and files, which can include student records. Ask whoever manages your school accounts before you connect one. Also check that the tool connects to the apps your school uses: the Gemini app, for example, has no Microsoft 365 connector.

Where to find it

  • Claude: Connectors. Microsoft 365 is read-only on school accounts unless an admin turns on more.
  • ChatGPT: Connected apps, added through Plugins. Older guides call them Connectors.
  • Gemini: Connected Apps. No Microsoft 365 connector.

Using it well

The habit that matters most.

The agreement trapWhen AI just agrees with you

Chatbots lean toward agreeing with you. Bring a half-formed opinion and they tend to back it up.

That's risky in advising. If you describe a student and the option you're leaning toward, the answer will often agree with you, and it feels like confirmation. A 2026 paper by Chandra and colleagues, Sycophantic Chatbots Cause Delusional Spiraling, Even in Ideal Bayesians, found that knowing about this bias doesn't stop it. Habits do.

  • Bring an idea, then ask for the other side. "I'm leaning toward the welding program for this student. Argue against it."
  • Ask what's wrong. "Tell me the weak spots in this plan."
  • Test fast agreement. Ask the opposite question in a new chat. If it agrees with both, you've learned something about the answer.

One hard rule: read everything before it goes to a student, a family, or an employer. AI can write the first draft. You write the last one.
