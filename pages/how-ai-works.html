How Do AI Chatbots Work?

AI comes in many forms - from recommendation systems to self-driving cars. Today we're focusing on AI chatbots, the tools you'll use most often in your work. The product names, features, and vendor comparisons on this page were verified against official sources in November 2026. Names and tier labels change often, so check the vendor before relying on a detail here.

The "Zip File of the Internet" Mental Model

Think of Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT as a "zip file" of the internet. During training, the AI reads essentially all of the internet and compresses that knowledge into its neural network. When you ask a question, it's recalling information from this compressed knowledge base.

This compression happens through a process called pre-training, where the model learns patterns from massive amounts of text. Then, through post-training, human labelers fine-tune it to respond helpfully as an assistant.

See what's in the training data: FineWeb Explorer lets you search through a sample of the actual web pages used to train AI models.

Prompts: How You Talk to AI

A prompt is simply what you type (or say) to the AI. It's your input - the question, instruction, or request that kicks off the conversation. Everything you've learned so far - tokens, context windows, modalities - all comes together in your prompt.

When you send a prompt:

  • Your text gets broken into tokens
  • Those tokens enter the AI's context window
  • Prompts can include other forms of data like images or files
  • The AI predicts the most helpful response based on patterns from its training
  • Some predictions will invoke the usage of external tools or plugins

The quality of AI's output depends heavily on the quality of your input. Vague prompts get vague answers. Specific, well-structured prompts get useful responses. This is why "prompt engineering" has become such a valuable skill.

Key insight: You're not searching a database or giving commands to a simple program. You're having a conversation with a system that predicts helpful responses. The more context and clarity you provide, the better it can help you.

Understanding Tokens: How AI Reads Text

AI doesn't read words the way we do. It breaks text into tokens - small chunks that can be parts of words, whole words, or punctuation. A short sentence might be 15 tokens, while a paragraph could be 50-100 tokens.

Why does this matter?

  • Longer conversations use more tokens, which can slow things down
  • There's a limit to how many tokens AI can process at once (the "context window")
  • Understanding tokens helps you write more efficient prompts

Try it yourself: Tik Tokenizer lets you see exactly how your text gets broken into tokens.

The Context Window: AI's Working Memory

When you chat with AI, you're building one running transcript together: your messages plus the AI's responses, in order. That running history is called the context window - the AI's working memory for your conversation.

  • AI remembers everything in your current chat
  • When you start a new chat, you wipe that memory and start fresh
  • Starting fresh is often good - it keeps the AI focused on your current task

The context window can be thought of like a short-term memory for the AI. It holds the recent conversation so the AI can respond appropriately, but it has a limited capacity.

Tip: Start a new chat when switching to a completely different topic. This prevents the AI from being distracted by old content and can improve response quality.

Visualize it: Context Window Size Visualizer lets you select different AI models and see exactly how much text fits in their context window - scroll through randomly generated content to experience the actual volume each model can process.

Probabilistic Recall: Why AI Can Be Wrong

AI doesn't "remember" facts like a database looks up records. It probabilistically reconstructs information based on patterns. This means:

  • It can occasionally get facts wrong (called "hallucinations")
  • It may sound confident even when incorrect
  • Common information is more reliable than obscure details
  • Always verify important information against primary sources

You've seen this before: Your phone's predictive text keyboard works the same way - it suggests words based on patterns, not by looking them up in a dictionary. AI chatbots are just a much larger version of this same concept.

Two Critical Limitations of Core LLMs

By default, when you talk to an AI, you're talking to a self-contained entity. There's no calculator, no internet connection, no access to your files - just a neural network predicting helpful responses based on its compressed knowledge. This creates two important limitations:

  • Knowledge cutoff dates - AI models are trained at a specific point in time, so they don't automatically know about recent news, current job postings, or today's market conditions. (See current cutoff dates)
  • Niche information gaps - AI excels at common knowledge but struggles with specialized, local information like specific company cultures, regional employer preferences, or your school's particular policies.

The good news? These limitations have solutions. Modern AI tools come equipped with additional capabilities that extend what they can do far beyond their base training...

Modalities: Different Ways to Interact

AI isn't limited to text. You can interact through voice and images too - but under the hood, everything still becomes tokens in that same context window.

Voice: Speak instead of type (tap the microphone icon). Your speech gets transcribed to text, converted to tokens, and processed like any typed message. ChatGPT Voice's "Live" option holds real-time spoken conversations, with limited use on the free plan - great for mock interview practice. (The older Advanced Voice retires on December 11, 2026, so guides that name it are out of date.)

Image Input: Upload photos and the AI "sees" them by converting the image into tokens (through a process of breaking it into patches). This works for text-based images like screenshots and documents, but also for identifying objects, plants, animals, landmarks, or anything visual - the AI analyzes what it sees, not just reads text from it.

File Upload (data): Upload files for the AI to analyze and extract information from. This can include documents, spreadsheets, and more, allowing for deeper interaction with your data.

Modality Best For
Text Most tasks - the default
Voice Input Quick queries, mobile use
Image Input Photos, screenshots
File Upload (data) Data analysis, report generation

Thinking Models: When AI Reasons Step by Step

Sometimes you need AI to work through a problem carefully rather than respond instantly. Thinking models (also called reasoning models) take extra time to "think" before answering - breaking complex problems into steps, checking their logic, and considering alternatives.

When you enable thinking mode:

  • The AI pauses before responding (seconds to minutes)
  • It works through the problem internally, similar to how you might talk yourself through a difficult decision
  • You often get more accurate answers for complex tasks

When thinking helps most:

  • Math and logic problems
  • Multi-step planning
  • Analyzing tradeoffs
  • Tasks where getting it right matters more than getting it fast

When to skip it:

  • Quick factual questions
  • Creative brainstorming
  • Simple tasks where speed matters

The tradeoff: Thinking takes longer and uses more resources. For straightforward questions, standard mode is faster and works fine. Save thinking mode for when you need the AI to really work through something.

Tools: Extending AI's Capabilities

Remember those limitations? Modern AI solves them by connecting to external tools. When the AI recognizes it needs outside help, it uses a tool, gets the result back, and continues.

Web Search: Bridges both the knowledge cutoff and niche information gaps by searching the internet in real-time. The AI formulates queries, retrieves pages, and synthesizes answers from current sources. Limitation: Not all websites allow AI tools to access their content, so some information may be unavailable.

Code Execution: Some tasks require actual computation rather than language prediction. When needed, the AI writes and runs code to perform calculations, analyze data, or create visualizations - things a language model alone cannot reliably do.

Tool What It Does
Web Search Searches internet for current information
Code Execution Runs code for calculations, data analysis, charts

Deep Research: Your AI Research Assistant

Deep Research isn't just another tool - it's a full research workflow. Instead of a quick web search, the AI becomes a research assistant that works for you over 5-30 minutes, running dozens of searches, reading through sources, and synthesizing findings into a comprehensive report.

How it works:

  • You describe what you want to research
  • The AI creates a research plan and asks clarifying questions
  • It runs 20-50+ web searches, reading and analyzing pages
  • It synthesizes everything into a structured report with citations

Best use cases for career services:

  • Labor market research - "Research entry-level marketing salaries in the Boston area, including trends and top employers"
  • Industry analysis - "What skills are most in-demand for data analytics roles right now?"
  • Employer research - "Compile information about [Company]'s culture, interview process, and recent news"
  • Resource compilation - "Find scholarship opportunities for first-generation college students in STEM"

Where to find it:

  • Claude: "Research". Click the + button below the message box and choose Research. Web search has to be on, and it is for paid plans.
  • ChatGPT: "Deep research". Choose it from the + (tools) menu, or type /Deepresearch. Limited on the free plan, with more on paid plans.
  • Gemini: "Deep Research" option in Google's Gemini. Limited for everyone, with higher limits on Google's paid AI plans.
  • Perplexity: "Research" (some menus call it Deep Research). Limited on the free plan.

The tradeoff: Deep Research takes real time (minutes, not seconds) and typically counts against usage limits more heavily. Use it when you need comprehensive research, not for quick questions.

Projects: Stop Re-Explaining Yourself

A Project is a folder of files plus standing instructions that every chat inside it already knows about. Instead of opening a blank chat and re-pasting who you are, which school you serve, and what you are working on, you open a chat that already has all of it.

Three things live in a Project, and only three:

  • Files. The ICAP template, the FAFSA checklist, last year's senior timeline, the employer contact list. Anything you would otherwise re-upload or re-paste. The AI reads across all of them on demand, so you do not have to point at a specific file in each message.
  • Instructions. A short standing brief every chat inherits. Good instructions cover who you are (a rootEd advisor in a rural high school), who the audience is (seniors, families, employers), the voice to write in, any hard rules you want held to, and what to do when something is unclear.
  • Chats. Every conversation you start inside a Project stays grouped with it. Come back two weeks later and the thread from FAFSA night prep is right there, already scoped. No scrolling a global chat list to find it.

Three shapes that fit advisor work: Projects are flexible enough that the hard part is picking a shape and sticking with it. These three cover most of a year.

Shape What You Load Into It Why It Pays Off
One per school year or senior cohort The ICAP template, the FAFSA checklist, last year's timeline, the calendar of deadlines you are working against. Every chat already knows which deadlines are live and what the year looked like last time. You start from the work instead of from the setup.
One per employer or partner relationship Site-visit notes, the contact history, what they offered last time (a job shadow, summer hires, a speaker for a career day). When you sit down to write the follow-up, the full history of that relationship is already in the room. Over a year it becomes real institutional memory for one partner.
One per recurring deliverable The last few versions of the newsletter or the counselor update, a short style note, anything the audience cares about. Every new draft starts in the right voice and the right structure instead of from a blank page.

One thing to remember: Projects are the single biggest habit change between casual chatbot use and getting real leverage out of this. The trap is that they look optional, so most people never create one and spend the whole year re-pasting the same context into fresh chats. If a body of work is going to come up more than twice, it deserves a Project.

Where to find it:

  • Claude: Projects. Available on every plan, including free.
  • ChatGPT: Projects, at near parity with Claude's. Project instructions override your global custom instructions.
  • Gemini: Notebooks. Open the sidebar and choose New notebook under "Notebooks." A notebook remembers its sources, instructions, and chats, which makes it the closest match to a Project. Since September 2026 it is available on school Google accounts too, on by default unless your district turns it off. Notebooks also sync with Gemini Notebook, the tool formerly called NotebookLM.

Artifacts: Things You Can Actually Use

When the AI makes something substantial, it does not bury it in the middle of a chat reply. It opens a panel next to the conversation. You keep talking on the left, the thing you are building lives on the right, and the two stay in sync. Ask for a change and it updates in place instead of scrolling by as another wall of text.

Documents, tables, and small working tools all land there the same way.

What You Ask For What Lands in the Panel Why It Beats a Chat Reply
A one-page FAFSA deadline tracker for your senior cohort A working page listing each deadline and the steps under it, that you can click through as students complete them. You can keep it open during a family night instead of re-reading a chat log to find the answer.
A parent-facing explainer on what the FAFSA asks for A clean document you can edit line by line and hand back for another pass. Revising is a conversation, not a re-prompt. The document keeps its shape while the wording changes.
A comparison of three postsecondary pathways for one student A table you can adjust, add a column to, and paste into your notes. It stays put while you talk through it, so the student is looking at one thing instead of chasing a scroll.

The point is the turnaround, not the artifact. A FAFSA deadline tracker is not a hard thing to want. It is a hard thing to get. "Can someone build me a simple tool for this" is normally a request that goes nowhere: no budget, no developer, no timeline, and by the time anyone could get to it the deadline has passed. Here it takes a sentence and about a minute. The bar for what is worth building drops to whatever is worth asking for.

The tradeoff: What comes back is a first draft, not a finished product. A generated tracker looks finished the moment it renders, and looking finished is not the same as being right. The dates and dollar figures inside it came from the model, not from your source, so check those against the real thing before you work from the tool.

Where to find it:

  • Claude: Artifacts. Opens on its own when the output warrants it.
  • ChatGPT: Writing blocks and code blocks: an editable draft or piece of code inside the reply, which you can open full screen. They replaced Canvas, which is no longer available on ChatGPT's current models, so older guides that mention Canvas are out of date.
  • Gemini: Canvas, invoked manually. Use Add Files, then Canvas.

Skills: Teach It Your Process Once

A Skill is a packaged set of instructions that turns the AI into a specialist for one task. You write it once, name it, and it gets used automatically whenever you describe matching work, instead of digging up the same long prompt every week.

Think of it as a folder the AI opens when the situation calls for it. Inside is a SKILL.md file: a short header naming the Skill and saying when to use it, then step-by-step instructions, the things you would tell a new colleague on day one. Reference material (a template, your deadline list, the format your counselor expects) goes in the same folder.

The part that surprises people: you do not have to invoke it. Once installed, the AI reads the description of every Skill you have and reaches for the matching one on its own. You describe the task out loud, "here are my notes from the hospital site visit, write the follow-up," and it runs your employer follow-up Skill.

Three worth building for advisor work. The test: if you have explained the same process twice, the third time should be a Skill.

Skill What You Write Into It What Changes Day to Day
Employer follow-up Your structure after a site visit: thank them, name what they offered, propose the next step, keep it short, and write to a plant manager, not a parent. Hand over your notes, get a sendable draft in your voice, and the follow-up goes out the same week.
Family-facing rewrite The rules you already apply by instinct: no acronyms on first use, name the deadline and the cost of missing it, plain language, and what to do for a family that reads in Spanish. Any notice, email, or FAFSA night handout gets the same treatment, consistent across the year, without rebuilding the instructions each time.
ICAP conversation prep What to pull from a student's file before a planning meeting: where they are against the plan, what changed, the few questions worth asking, and a note to flag anything it is inferring rather than reading. Prep happens in the two minutes before the meeting instead of the twenty you do not have.

You do not have to write it from scratch. Describe the workflow and ask the AI to draft the SKILL.md; the first draft is usually close enough that you are correcting rather than composing. You install it through your tool's own settings, and since that location moves as vendors change their interfaces, search the tool's current docs if you cannot find it.

Where to find it:

  • Claude: Skills. Auto-invoked when your request matches the Skill's description. Available on every plan, including free.
  • ChatGPT: Skills, now found under Plugins in the sidebar. Also auto-invoked on a description match, or picked yourself with an @ mention. The catch is not capability, it is access: OpenAI lists them for Business, Enterprise, Healthcare, and Edu workspaces, subject to admin settings.
  • Gemini: Gems, for now. Google is replacing Gems with skills that Gemini uses on its own when they fit, starting November 2026 for personal Google accounts and in 2027 for school accounts. Until your school account switches over, a Gem is manual: a specialist you have to remember to switch into, which is one you will forget to use.

Connectors: Ask About the Real Thing

A connector gives the AI authenticated access to an app your work already lives in. Once one is set up, you can ask questions about the actual source material instead of pasting screenshots, re-uploading a file you already have, or describing something from memory and hoping you got it right.

Two of them cover most of an advisor's week.

Connector What It Reaches What It Unlocks
Google Drive Docs, sheets, and slides from your Google Drive. The folders you do not own. A shared counselor drive, a district folder, materials a partner organization hosts. Instead of clicking through it the night before to work out what changed, you ask.
Microsoft 365 Outlook mail and calendar, OneDrive, and SharePoint, from one setup. The day you are actually having. "What is on my calendar tomorrow, and for each meeting, what was the last thing we said to each other" is one question instead of twenty minutes of inbox archaeology.

Connectors can act, not just read. Claude's Google connectors can send, reply to, and forward Gmail, create and change Calendar events, and share, move, or trash Drive files. By default it asks for your approval before each of those. Its Microsoft 365 connector is read-only on a school account unless an administrator turns on write tools. ChatGPT's apps can also take actions; in Edu and Enterprise workspaces an admin turns apps and their actions on, and new ones start off. So do not assume any connector only reads. Check what the one you just turned on is actually allowed to do before you point it at anything that matters, and read every approval request before you click it.

If your district runs Microsoft, this decides the tool. The Gemini app has no Microsoft 365 connector at all. Not a limited one: no Outlook, no OneDrive, no SharePoint. (Google's separate, paid Gemini Enterprise product does connect to Microsoft 365, but that is not the Gemini your district's Google account gives you.) Claude and ChatGPT both reach Outlook and SharePoint. For an advisor whose mail, calendar, and every shared folder live in the Microsoft stack, that is not a feature-comparison footnote, it is most of the value of connectors disappearing. It is also the kind of thing you find out after you have committed, so find it out now.

This is a district IT decision, not a personal one. Connectors touch school email and school files, which means they touch student records. Whether you are allowed to turn one on is often settled by district policy and your IT administrator, and on a school-managed device the setting may not be yours to change. Ask before you connect, not after. The answer may be yes, and it is a much better conversation to have in advance than to explain afterward.

Where to find it:

  • Claude: Connectors.
  • ChatGPT: Connected apps, formerly called Connectors, now added through Plugins. Older walkthroughs still call them Connectors, so search both names.
  • Gemini: Connected Apps (older guides call them Extensions). No Microsoft 365 connector of any kind.

The Agreement Trap: When AI Just Agrees With You

Sycophancy is a failure mode of its own, separate from hallucination. A hallucination tells you something false about the world. Sycophancy tells you something flattering about your own thinking. These models have a built-in bias toward agreeing with whoever they are talking to. Come in with a half-formed opinion and it tends to validate it. Come in with a wrong one and it often validates that too.

A recent paper from Chandra et al. (Feb 2026), Sycophantic Chatbots Cause Delusional Spiraling, Even in Ideal Bayesians, shows this pattern traps even a careful, rational person, not just a careless one. The most uncomfortable finding in the paper is that simply knowing about sycophancy is not enough to escape it. The authors expect awareness to reduce how often people spiral, but not to eliminate it: the users in their study who suspected they were being flattered kept spiraling anyway. Awareness alone is not the fix. Habit is.

This section is here because of the work you do. An advisor talking a student through a pathway decision is exactly the person who gets quietly validated by a model that agrees with whatever it is handed. You are already leaning somewhere. You describe the student, you describe the option you have in mind, and the answer comes back agreeing with you. Nothing about it feels wrong. It feels like confirmation. The cost of a validated bad plan is a student's year.

Three habits do the work that awareness alone cannot.

Come in with an idea, not a blank question. Instead of "what should this student do?" try "I am leaning toward the welding program for this student for these reasons. Argue the other side." A blank question hands the model nothing to push against, so it fills the space by agreeing with the last thing you said. An idea gives it something to hit.

Ask for pushback explicitly. "Tell me what is wrong with this plan, not what is right about it." Flip the default orientation on the turns where it matters. You are not being rude to it, you are correcting for a bias you know is there.

Treat fast agreement as a signal to probe. If it agrees with you flatly and fast, ask it to argue the opposite view as strongly as it can, or paste the reversed question into a fresh chat and see whether it also agrees with that. If it agrees with both sides, you just learned something: not about the student, about the answer you were given.

The one hard rule: never send AI output to a student, a family, or an employer without reading it yourself. It can write the first draft. It cannot write the last one.

Everything here applies to every large language model, not one product. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and anything else trained the same way share this bias. It is a trait of how they are built, not a quirk of one tool, so the habits port over exactly as written.
